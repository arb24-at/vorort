# VorOrt Berlin — Design Direction: Tangible Order

> **Status:** External design reference only. This is not an approved production specification, page template, roadmap, or source of truth. Keep only the ideas that improve the VorOrt visual system.

## Core idea

**Tangible Order** combines two qualities:

- **Tangible:** real spaces, materials, devices and physical IT work. Prefer recognisable Berlin homes, home offices, workplaces, cable routes, network equipment, desks, storage and everyday technology over generic "tech" imagery.
- **Order:** professional clarity. Layout, diagrams and imagery should make complicated technology feel understandable, organised and deliberate.

The useful distinction is not a new beige/brown visual identity. VorOrt should keep its existing recognisable design system and use Tangible Order mainly as a principle for **subject matter, spatial thinking and information visualisation**.

Avoid the two category clichés:

- generic tech-company visuals: blue gradients, abstract nodes, circuit patterns, floating cloud/cyber graphics;
- cheap local repair-shop visuals: cartoon icons, excessive friendliness, device-repair imagery and cluttered service menus.

## Shared family

Homepage, Private and Business pages should clearly belong to the same website through shared typography, navigation, core palette, spacing logic, contact patterns, illustration quality and brand voice.

Audience differences should come primarily from **composition, spatial scale, subject matter, density and geometry**, not recolouring the same component set.

### Private character

- room- and device-scale: apartment, desk, shelf, home office, living room;
- recognisable everyday situations;
- more breathing room and shorter text blocks;
- softer spatial compositions and slightly looser geometry;
- environmental or editorial illustration rather than technical schematics;
- language centred on *your home, your Wi-Fi, your devices*.

### Business character

- workplace- and infrastructure-scale: rooms, workspaces, cable routes, access points, cabinets, storage and shared services;
- more structured and precise spatial relationships;
- moderately higher information density;
- diagrams may become more technical, but must remain understandable to an owner or office manager;
- language centred on *your office, your team, your infrastructure*.

## The 3-second opening rule

For every important page, the **Hero + first section after the Hero** should let a visitor understand within roughly three seconds:

1. what the page is about;
2. what broad kinds of help it includes;
3. whether it is probably relevant to them.

This must work mostly through composition and visual recognition, not through reading several paragraphs or many small labels.

The first section after the Hero must add a **new kind of information**. It should not feel like a second Hero.

## Opening concepts worth retaining

These are useful concepts to experiment with, not fixed production layouts.

### Homepage

**Hero:** broad, problem-led on-site IT help in Berlin. Avoid forcing an immediate Private-versus-Business choice.

**First visual section:** show the breadth of where and how VorOrt helps through recognisable environments or situations rather than an icon/service grid. The visitor should quickly recognise that the service covers home, home-office and small-business contexts.

The homepage is the bridge between the two page families, so it should remain the easiest and least technical entrance.

### Private Home Network

**Hero:** clearly identifies home network / Wi-Fi / NAS help for homes and home offices.

**First visual section:** communicate **Wi-Fi coverage, cabling, storage and connected devices as one home environment**, not four separate product cards. A spatial home illustration, annotated environment or similarly integrated composition is a useful direction.

The key message is that customers do not need to diagnose the solution themselves; they can recognise the situation and understand that the network is planned around real rooms and everyday use.

### Business Network Infrastructure

**Hero:** clearly identifies network infrastructure for small and growing workplaces in Berlin.

**First visual section:** communicate **workspace, cabling, network equipment, business Wi-Fi, storage/services and access as one infrastructure system**. A structured floor/workplace view, layered system or similarly coherent composition is a useful direction.

The visual should demonstrate planning competence without becoming an enterprise datacentre schematic.

## Useful visualisation vocabulary

Choose the visual form according to the information relationship. Do not force every item into a card or list.

| Information relationship | Useful forms |
| --- | --- |
| Recognition / atmosphere | Environmental image, detail image, situational illustration |
| Scope / breadth | One integrated environment, territory composition, annotated scene |
| Connected system | Spatial diagram, topology, layered environment |
| Sequence / change | Transformation, staged path, before → change → outcome |
| Customer situation | Editorial vignette, dominant scene with supporting details |
| Technical method | Structured framework or system diagram when it genuinely adds understanding |
| Capability depth | Asymmetrical editorial spread; not every capability needs equal visual weight |
| Boundary | Quiet contrast section, supported/specialist split, contextual note |
| Trust / proof | Strong factual statement, restrained editorial block, genuine evidence |
| Location | Useful Berlin/environmental context where it supports the on-site proposition |
| Conversion | Clear Phone / WhatsApp / Form paths without pressure tactics |

Photography or illustration should carry real explanatory value. Do not add diagrams simply because the subject is technical.

## Page rhythm

A long page should vary visual weight and representation. A useful rhythm might move between:

**visual recognition → explanation → system/detail → quieter prose → proof/boundary → continuation/contact**

This is a principle, not a mandatory section sequence.

Short sections are allowed to remain short. Paragraphs are allowed to remain paragraphs. Lists are appropriate when the information genuinely is a list.

## What to avoid

- Hero followed by another Hero-sized heading/diagram composition.
- Long small-text explanations immediately after the Hero.
- Generic icon grids as the main service-scope explanation.
- Repeated equal card grids.
- Several consecutive sections built from heading + bullets/rows.
- Accordions or tabs hiding information needed to decide whether the service is relevant.
- Tiny technical diagrams that require close inspection.
- Reusing dark software-dashboard panels for unrelated subjects.
- Using colour alone to distinguish Private and Business.
- Making Private childish or Business enterprise-corporate.
- Generic stock imagery: smiling technician, headset agent, handshake, glowing server room, cyber shield, hooded hacker.
- Decorative diagrams with no explanatory purpose.
- Padding thin content merely to make sections equal height.

## Mobile rules

Mobile must preserve the idea rather than merely stack the desktop layout.

- Simplify complex environments and diagrams instead of shrinking them unreadably.
- Keep the primary visual message visible without hover or interaction.
- Recompose spatial diagrams into a clear overview plus a few readable details when necessary.
- Preserve Private softness and Business precision through layout, not just colour.
- Avoid turning desktop compositions into long stacks of identical cards.
- Keep contact actions comfortably tappable and obvious.

## What remains useful from Tangible Order

Retain these ideas as inspiration for future Design Lab work:

- physical, recognisable environments rather than generic tech abstraction;
- "order" expressed through clear spatial relationships and hierarchy;
- Private at room/device scale versus Business at workplace/infrastructure scale;
- one integrated visual for connected service scope where appropriate;
- visual form chosen by meaning rather than by component availability;
- a strong three-second comprehension test for the Hero and first section.

Do **not** treat the previous full-page wireframes, fixed seven-section architectures, exact photographic prescriptions, typography micro-adjustments, or generated DeepSeek HTML/CSS as approved direction. Those were exploratory material only.
